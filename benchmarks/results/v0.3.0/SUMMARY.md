**Reading the tables.** *Wall* is the median time to replay the whole trace over 10 timed runs (after 2 warm-up runs), with the interquartile range and the range. *p99 / p99.9 / max* are per-operation latencies from one further run, timer included. *RSS Δ* is how much the resident set grew during one replay in a fresh process; *frag* is RSS Δ divided by the trace's peak live requested bytes (1.0 would be perfect). Allocators other than `orisnik`/`orisnitsa` cannot `resize` in place or `purge`, so those operations are no-ops for them.

## mimalloc-bench

[mimalloc-bench](https://github.com/daanx/mimalloc-bench) at commit `391382606681` with Oris added as two `LD_PRELOAD` shims (`benchmarks/mimalloc-bench/`): `orisnik` and `orisnitsa`. Ubuntu 24.04.4 LTS, AMD Ryzen 9 5950X 16-Core Processor, 4 logical cores, 8 GB RAM; commit `426a005`, 2026-10-22T15:37:29Z. Median of the runs.

**Read the threaded rows with care.** Oris is a single-threaded allocator; its shims put one global lock around every call, so a multithreaded test (marked †) measures Oris *serialized*, not a multithreaded Oris (that is v2.x on the roadmap). The shims also round every request up to 16 bytes, the alignment `malloc` owes its callers.

**Rows marked ‡ run for a fixed time.** mimalloc-bench reports their *time* as a relative time derived from throughput (lower is better, as for elapsed time), and their *peak RSS* grows with the work done in that time — so a slower allocator can look leaner.
